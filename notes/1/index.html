Three tax problems to price before you close

Your QoE probably doesn't cover tax. Here's what that can cost on a small deal, and how to put a number on it before you sign.

November 28, 2026 · From Lockwood

Most QoE engagements on small deals leave tax out of scope. Check yours. If tax isn't listed, nobody on your side is looking at it, and tax problems tend to show up at the closing table or a year after closing.

Here's a made-up deal with three common problems.

The deal (illustrative)
  • HVAC company, $4M purchase price
  • 12 field techs paid on 1099s
  • Does installs in two states, files sales tax in one
  • S corporation, and the LOI says stock purchase

1. The techs on 1099s

If those techs should have been W-2 employees, the company may owe back payroll taxes, plus penalties and interest. In a stock deal, that stays with the company you just bought.

What to ask: how the techs are scheduled, supervised and paid, and whether the company has ever had a payroll audit or a worker file for unemployment.

2. Sales tax in the second state

Selling into a state can create a duty to collect its sales tax, even with no office there. If the company never registered, that state can come back for the tax it should have collected, usually out of the company's pocket.

What to ask: sales by state for each of the last few years, and whether installs or materials are taxable in that state.

3. The S corp and the stock purchase

In a stock deal, the company's tax history comes with it. If the S election was ever broken (for example, by an ineligible shareholder or a second class of stock), the company could be treated as a C corp for those years. Structure matters here: an asset deal or an F reorganization changes who carries that risk.

What to ask: the S election, every shareholder since it was made, the operating or shareholder agreements, and any distributions that weren't pro rata.

Pricing it before you sign

For each problem, you want two numbers: what it costs to fix now, and what you'd hold back if it isn't fixed. With those, you can ask for a price cut, an escrow, a specific indemnity, or a fix before closing. Without them, you're negotiating on a feeling.

Your CPA and attorney decide what to do. The point is to get them the facts early, while you still have leverage.

Selling instead?

Same three problems, other side of the table. A buyer's diligence is likely to find them. If you find them first, you can fix them or price them yourself, before they turn into a holdback.
